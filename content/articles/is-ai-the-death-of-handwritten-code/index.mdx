Last week, [David Heinemeier Hansson jumped on stage at Rails World 2026 to speak about his new found love for agentic coding][dhh]. You probably heard about it - the creator of a prolific project, shouting about how you no longer need said prolific project is sure to generate some headlines.

[dhh]: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vDjW_dRyKXY

His keynote kicked off a week of intense introspection for me. DHH and many others in the industry are selling a new era of coding, characterised by endlessly prompting machines without the need to deeply understand the code. The world of "Pencils Down"[^1]. Many consider this an inevitability, but is it actually worth buying what they're selling?

[^1]: For an interesting take on Pencils Down, contrary to the world I'm outlining here, I strongly recommend this article: https://intertwingly.net/blog/2026/09/25/Pencils-Down-Notation-Up.html

## Walk before you run

AI fanatics are screaching about moving faster. Move faster they say, and ship more features, forget the code. Degrade our attention spans by spinning ten plates at once. Cut out our coworkers and lose all sense of companionship - you can move faster by ignoring their advice and listening to AI instead - it answers now and with better advice than our coworkers. Now, now, now. There's no room for juniors, no room for mentorship. Yuck. Ship more value for stakeholders. Redundancies, redunancies.

  - **The team**:
    - People without a programming background writing code is a double edged sword. It's great that they're enabled to create now - but what impact does this have on programmers?
    - Lazy engineers (read all engineers) tempted to blindly take ticket output -> agent input -> code review. They throw their coworkers under the brain bus.  

## Reach taller, don't run faster

To me, at it's core, DHH is telling you to run faster. To produce more features. To embrace the slop ("take the fucking black pill"), and forget the code underneath. I think you should reach taller instead. Leverage AI to research more deeply, to architect more, to reason more, to make throwaway prototypes, to build comprehensive testing suites, to engineer. The future will be filled with low effort tools that do everything. If you can't be bothered to write, than I can't be bothered to read. NOTHING HAS CHANGED. We can keep choosing to make great tools. To show up every day and rage against the monoty. Do not let your critical thinking atrophy. Do not let your relationships fade. Coding is social.

We haven't hired a junior at my workplace in years.

We are increasingly encouraged in our lives to move faster - watch shorter videos, be more productive. What about the human side of coding?

2. We're being sold a narrative about productivity, but do our experiences actually align with this narrative? We might be spewing out code quicker, but only if we sacrifice our understanding. The human mind is the bottle neck. What ever happened to measure twice cut once? 
  - DHH's own book, Rework, which explores some of these ideas and runs counter to what DHH is saying today. "Throw as much code and features as the screen as possible".
3. This isn't just resistance to change - I understand the appeal, and I think with some restraint we could use a tool like this to great benefit. How and why
4. The hidden costs to AI.
  - **The product**:
    - Adding features is basically free. In DHH/Jason Fried's book Rework, their own words, what makes a product great is just as much what's not added. Do we all end up driving the code equivalent of Homer Simpson's car?
    - Non-bvious issues, hiding beneath the surface. AI can produce code that looks great, even to a code review, but hides nasty misinterpratations of business logic or user behaviour that are difficult to debug.
  - **The profession**:
    - Programming used to be methodical and slow. The act of coding encapsulated the act of thinking.
  - At my workplace, a schism is emerging. There are those that embrace agentic coding. Running several worktrees in parallel, building features faster than ever before, and rewriting large swaths of code bases. These individuals are embracing "Pencils Down" - and pointing to bottle necks in code review and QA as the only thing holding them up from being more efficient than ever before. There are other individuals that are using AI to improve the quality of the work they produce, rather than the quantity. "As a code reviewer, I should not be able to tell the difference between human written and AI written code.
6. Derive the way forward. Recommendations should feel earned.
  - Authors need to remain accountable for their code, now more than ever before.
  - Use AI to understand new domains, rapidly explore code bases at high level, diagnose bugs, prototype, build internal tooling, and complete prelimary code reviews. Use your brain to solve problems and **handwrite code**. No matter how tempting, we must handwrite as much code as possible.
  7. The truth is I don't know what the best way forward is. But I do think we shouldn't be too eager to throw away everything we've learnt over decades of software engineering, all for the promise of things being "easier". We listen too much to the reward centre of our brain, and not enough to the curiosity, to the joy of solving problems, to the beauty of doing things the hard way.
