I love programming.

I've been doing it professionally for almost a decade now, and just for the hell
of it another five years before that. I've spoken passionately on podcasts and
given motivational talks to prospective university students. I've debated
architectural paradigms, I've written articles and I've devoted countless hours
to side projects that never got off the ground.

I've loved every bit of it. It's safe to say that at this point, programming is
not just a part of my identity; it's who I am. 

Or rather, it's who I was.

The software industry is undergoing an era of rapid change, spurred by the
introduction of agentic coding models. Their adoption has been widespread,
swift, and polarising - in a process that has gradually erroded my love for
a once proud profession. Each week it feels as though developers are competing
to throw away more and more of what we've built. Is the software industry in a
race to the bottom? 

## Productivity above all

One of the greatest parts of software development is its depth. You can spend a
lifetime learning - incrementally building deeper understanding. Topics like
memory management, database design, networking, accessibility, algorithms,
compiler design, caching... the list goes on. If you somehow ran out of
interesting things to work on in software you'd be the first in human history.

Learning how to be productive in a domain with so much depth takes years of
experience. Graduate developers, for example, are often a drain on resources
while they learn how contribute effectively (though they often grow quickly too).

The great and terrible thing about agentic coding is that it allows you to
contribute on topics without the need to deeply understand them. A cursory
understanding of web accessibility, for example, is enough to build out a
complex UI with great keyboard and screen reader support, whilst making sure the
agent doesn't go off track or break some unrelated feature.

Sure, someone who spent years studying accessible interface design, gaining
hands on experience with screen readers, and engaging in user testing sessions
for people with disabilities might be able to push their agent a little
further - but the gap has shrunk dramatically and only continues to shrink more
as time goes on.

For the executive, this is a massive win. ["The utopia is almost here"][utopia] they say.
And don't get me wrong, for the developer who only wants to be more productive,
to ship more features and build more apps, this is also a massive win.

[utopia]: https://youtu.be/vDjW_dRyKXY?si=XHpWVrplr9jjN4HP&t=3640

But what if, like me, you actually see the enormity of software development as a
feature, not a bug. For someone that loves to learn and grow, someone that loves
to be challenged, to solve problems, and to invest the time to put yourself in
other peoples' shoes - there's never been a greater loss in the history of
programming.

We're trading understanding for productivity at an industrial scale.

## "Just don't use it then"

If only it were that simple.

Programming is a social sport. Code is not written in a vacuum. We have
teammates and colleages, maintainers and contributors, and even your past self
who may as well be a foreign entity. Not to mention the giants' shoulders on which
we stand - the complex graph of libraries and tools that we all build apon.
Software products are a carefully stacked tower of many people's contributions. 

You may choose the long road of understanding, but will everyone else?

To make matters worse, agentic code has a tendency to spread once introduced.
Agents can code much faster than humans, shipping rapidly at the cost of
complexity and architectural fragility. "Add this feature", "fix that bug",
"write those tests", "rewrite it in Rust". All it takes is one prompt, and the
monkey's paw will curl yet again.

As the bloat grows, it becomes more and more tempting to pick up an agent to
help you sift through it all. That agent will help you along, but only make the
problem worse.

You could take steps to slow this bloat, and many do, but they too will soon
fall behind those that can slop at the speed of sound. Before you know it the
code base has become monolithic - too large for any mere human to truly
understand. 

This is the real race to the bottom. Agentic adoption slowly squeezes out anyone
pushing for software quality.

## Maybe we can change their mind?

Okay, so you're as naive as I am. Game designers already understand how hard it
is to remove a tool from the toolbox. It's not possible to close the can of worms.
Consider this quote from game designer Soren Johnson, famous for his work on the
Civilization franchise:

> "Given the opportunity, players will optimize the fun out of a game. [...]
> The greatest danger is that once a player discovers such an exploit, she will
> never be able to play the game again without using it – the knowledge cannot
> be ignored or forgotten, even if the player wishes otherwise."

---

A growing idea in the agentic coding world is "pencils down". As times goes on,
developers becoming decreasingly concerned with the content that the agent
produces, instead focused on the outcome. The less you read, the less you
comprehend, the faster you can move. Moving to progressively to higher levels
of abstraction over time.

{/* This is the true race to the bottom. The dev that moves the fastest, understands the least pushes the others out. */}

In this world, there is no longer room for organic developer. You'll drown in
agentic slop, your changes will be overwritten. You'll receive pull requests that
rewrite it all in Rust.

The other problem is something that game designers have understood for at least
15 years: it's _really_ hard to not use a tool that makes your life easier, even
if that tool makes you miserable. Consider this quote from game designer Soren
Johnson, famous for his work on the Civilization franchise:

> "Given the opportunity, players will optimize the fun out of a game. [...]
> The greatest danger is that once a player discovers such an exploit, she will
> never be able to play the game again without using it – the knowledge cannot
> be ignored or forgotten, even if the player wishes otherwise."

## The end of an open web

- A free and open web. I messed around with Java, but HTML/CSS/JS is what really
  drew me in. The fact anyone could pick up these technologies and build a
  website was like a religious experience. Then I discovered this enormous open
  source community, people building in the open and sharing their work for the
  love of the game. It was incredible.
- Startup acquisitions of open source projects.
- Open source maintainers overwhelmed by a tidal wave of slop. A role that was
  already thankless has now become even more so. No wonder maintainers are so
  eager to accept an exit strategy from corporations.
- On the other hand
  - AI assisted reverse-engineering shows promise. It's early days,
    but the democrotisation of software is definitely a good thing for humanity.
    Giving the power of software to individuals rather than corporations - fuck
    yea. But as a professional in that industry? That's scary. There's two sides
    to that coin. Only time will tell.
  - However, in doing so, I do worry we are putting all our money into an even
    smaller handful of companies. Companies lead by individuals with shady
    backgrounds. Did you know Sam Altman did X? Did you know Anthropic did Y?

## Stuck in a silo

- Multi-disciplinary collaboration. Working beside great minds in design,
  engineering, and product. You had great people with different skills working
  towards a common but monolithic goal. Someone at your company was a screen
  reader pro, and another could string together playwright tests like no one else. Oh, and Pete in products knows more about the quirks of this database than anyone.
- Mentoring is dead, pair programming is dead - just ask your agent. Cross team communication is dead - just ask your agent to read the docs. We're more silo'd
  than ever before.
- Why have QA engineers when agents can write and run playwright tests? Why have
  designers when product people can prompt an agent? Does this continue to extend
  further as coding models improve?
- Agents write emails, agents read emails. Agents write docs, agents read docs. Agents review PRs, then agents fix the problems they find. We've even automated how we communicate to each other.
