There's a growing sentiment amongst some engineers in my workplace that the introduction of agentic coding has sapped all joy from our once proud profession. Some point to a lack of truly novel problems to solve, whilst others blame a torrent of comically large pull requests piling up on our proverbial desks. There's talk of career change and resignation - and can anyone actually remember the last time we hired a junior developer?

As a backdrop to these problems, AI influencers will tell you that this is all just pesimism! That change is inevitable and that we must embrace it with open arms. ["There's only one play here and it's total fucking optimism" says DHH][dhh]. "The utopia is almost here" he exclaims.

But how have we become so quick to throw away everything we've been building over the last few decades?

[dhh]: https://youtu.be/vDjW_dRyKXY?si=XHpWVrplr9jjN4HP&t=3640

## How did this happen?

Programmers are far from the first to discover that gaining more power doesn't lead to happiness. Consider this quote from game designer Soren Johnson, back in 2011:

> "Given the opportunity, players will optimize the fun out of a game. [...] The greatest danger is that once a player discovers such an exploit, she will never be able to play the game again without using it – the knowledge cannot be ignored or forgotten, even if the player wishes otherwise." [^1]

Programmers are not unlike players - except we have been trained over many years to optimise at all costs. To search for the most powerful tools, to use the most efficient algorithms. So is it any surprise we could throw ourselves into a pit of despair with such ferocity?

Our role relies on a contradiction: we are tasked with solving problems that no one else can - so what are we left with when there are no problems left to solve?

Without problem solving, we lose the foundation of what kept us interested. Without motivation, our industry faces an existential crisis - the only survivors of which are the fanatics, getting joy out of the act of taking over. But this too will be short lived.

Game designers have a big advantage over us: they can design. They can adjust the options available to players, adding or removing mechanics to create an experience that is hard to optimise the joy out of. We don't have that luxury in the real world.

## Efficiency above all

There's no debating that agentic coding can make you a more efficient developer. But much like catching an Uber in the middle of a marathon, or paying someone to grind your Path of Exile account to a top 100 spot on the leaderboard - finishing faster is not always better.

- We've turned ourselves into one mand bands: running several worktrees in parallel, building features faster than ever before, and rewriting large swaths of code bases - only to dump giant code reviews onto our poor coworkers. Shipping every feature that comes to mind. Building the homer simpson car.
- Observing bottle necks in code review and QA as the only thing holding them up from being more efficient than ever before.

## Won't somebody think of the children?

- It's more important than ever to invest in and grow our teammates. Agentic coding has silod us. Why wait for your coworker to weigh in when you can ask AI and get an answer now? Why invest in growing juniors when an agent can do everything they can?
- It's shortsighted. It's sacrificing the long term. If we're not growing new engineers what are we even doing?
- Our code review burden is astronomical. Some may point to AI as the answer here as well, but is it not important to truly understand the work that we're shipping? It's offloading the cognitive component of coding, the part we actually all enjoy to agents.

## Choose life

In a world where executives, algorithms, and tech influencers keep telling us to move faster - choose to think more. Choose to plan more, to prototype more, to research more. Choose to reach higher, rather than to always move faster. Choose to keep humanity and curiosity at the center of your work. To be clear - I'm not arguing against using AI. Just that we should be cautious how we use this hammer that makes everything look like a nail.

Invest in your team mates. Don't get caught up in a race to the bottom.
